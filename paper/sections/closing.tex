\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

Everything here is toy-scale: a 1.27M-parameter transformer, $8\times32\times32$ grayscale synthetic clips, a static camera, CPU only.
We have not run an open video model, so nothing here shows that mean-tying helps at scale; the argument that it should rests on the theory, which does not depend on scale, and on the network-free checks.
Each model variant was trained once, and the regulariser was tried at two weights only; it lowers the texture of the model's samples, and we do not know whether that can be avoided.
The errata on the permanence score come from our re-implementation of the printed equations, not from the authors' code, and we did not rebuild their benchmark; items marked \textbf{C} in Table~\ref{tab:errata} are concerns, not findings.
We propose no replacement score.
The classifier two-sample test is a lower bound on distinguishability.
Speedups are analytic FLOP counts; the timing check in \S\ref{sec:trained} covers only a small model on a CPU.

\section{Conclusion}

The base paper asked whether a shared-noise sample is as good as an independent-noise one.
For noise shared by copying, the answer is no, for a reason that can be stated in one sentence: the frame mean of $F$ white samples has variance $1/F$, and the network knows it.
Sharing by averaging respects that, is exact for an ideal denoiser, and costs nothing.
On a trained network the missing ingredient is the invariance that the ideal denoiser has for free.
Once it is taught, frame sharing does what the base paper's headline claimed, this time against the right reference, and a classifier cannot tell its samples from full compute.
What remains open is the price of that invariance in sample quality, the static-camera assumption, a reliable static map early in the trajectory, and above all scale.
