\section{Introduction}

A recent technical report, \emph{Entropy-Continuum Mechanics for Video}~\cite{garlapati2026ecm} (the \emph{base paper} below), treats the information in a video as a material that moves with the scene.
It draws two tools from that picture.
The first is a training-free \emph{permanence violation score} (PVS) that looks for information created away from occlusion boundaries.
The second is \emph{frame-shared generation}: in a video diffusion transformer, the tokens of a static background are computed in one leader frame and copied to the others, with a log-multiplicity attention bias (proportional attention~\cite{bolya2023tome}) standing in for the removed tokens.
The report is candid about its scale and its failures, and it ends on an open question: sharing forces the copies of a static token to start from the same noise, this ``yields a different sample'', and ``whether the shared-noise sample is as good is untested''.

This follow-up does two things.

\paragraph{Errata (\S\ref{sec:errata}).}
We list 17 errors, evaluation flaws and gaps in the base paper and verify most of them numerically.
The most consequential one concerns the headline result of frame sharing.
Its fidelity of 41.5--43.8\,dB is measured against full compute from noise that the method has already modified, while every baseline it is compared with is measured against the original output.
Against the original output the base paper's own number is 11--15\,dB, below every baseline in its table.

\paragraph{An answer to the open question (\S\ref{sec:theory}--\ref{sec:trained}).}
Copying one frame's noise to $F$ frames is not a neutral way to make tokens identical.
The $F$ copies of a static token are $F$ noisy measurements of one quantity, and the network has learned to pool them.
The pooled measurement is their \emph{mean}, whose noise is $\sqrt F$ times weaker than that of any single copy: static tokens are colder.
Handing the network one copy while telling it, through proportional attention, that it holds $F$ of them makes it over-confident by $\sqrt F$.
We show:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Theory.} For an ideal denoiser and a static region, replacing the copies by their frame mean leaves the generated sample \emph{exactly} unchanged, at the start or at any later step (Theorem~\ref{thm:mean}). Replacing them by one copy runs the sampler with noise $\sqrt F$ too strong; for Gaussian content the output variance is inflated by exactly $F$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:copy}).
\item \textbf{Network-free checks.} In an exactly solvable Gaussian model the law holds to three digits. With an exact nonlinear denoiser, copying returns the original clip in $\IdealCopySame\,\%$ of runs and averaging in $\IdealMeanSame$--$\IdealMeanSixSame\,\%$.
\item \textbf{A trained transformer.} Copying over-disperses backgrounds $\ACopyStd\times$ and lands $\ACopyOrig$\,dB from the original sample; averaging removes the over-dispersion and reaches $\AMeanOrig$\,dB.
\item \textbf{What does not work.} On a network trained in the standard way, averaging is not sample-preserving either: tied tokens are outside its training distribution and it answers with $\AMeanTex\times$ too much texture. On an equal footing, frame sharing then loses to simply taking fewer steps, with either way of tying. The base paper's central comparison does not survive.
\item \textbf{What makes it work.} The ideal denoiser ignores the per-frame deviations; a trained one does not. A 3k-step fine-tune with a regulariser that penalises this dependence (Eq.~\ref{eq:reg}) makes mean-tied sharing reproduce the original sample at $\BMeanDraftOrig$\,dB with $\BDraftSpeedup\times$ fewer FLOPs using the base paper's 2-step draft map, and at $\BMeanOrig$\,dB with $\BOracleSpeedup\times$ using an oracle map; the fewer-steps baseline is at $\BFewerTen$\,dB and $\BFewerSeven$\,dB for $2\times$ and $2.9\times$. Copying stays at $\BCopyOrig$\,dB. The regulariser is not free: the fine-tuned model's samples lose texture (Table~\ref{tab:variants}).
\end{itemize}
The change to the base method is one line of code, and it leaves the compute saving untouched.
All experiments are toy-scale and CPU-only, like those of the base paper; we report them as evidence about a mechanism.
