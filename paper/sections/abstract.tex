\noindent\textbf{Abstract.}\hspace{0.4em}A recent report, \emph{Entropy-Continuum Mechanics for Video}, treats video information as a moving material and derives a permanence score and \emph{frame-shared generation}, in which a video diffusion transformer computes static-background tokens once and copies them across frames.
We audit the report and build on it.
The audit lists 17 errors and gaps, most of them verified numerically.
The score charges pure noise $\NullBias$ bits per pixel that it states are free; it is unbounded and scales inversely with a clip's grain; its clip-level maximum grows with clip length; proportional attention is exact only without temporal position information; and the headline fidelity of frame sharing, 41.5--43.8\,dB, is measured against a reference the method has itself altered, while against the original output the report's own figure is 11--15\,dB, below every baseline.
Our new work answers the report's open question: is a sample generated from shared noise as good?
Not when the noise is shared by copying.
The $F$ copies of a static token are $F$ measurements of one quantity, the network pools them, and one measurement counted $F$ times makes it over-confident by $\sqrt F$.
We prove that for an ideal denoiser, replacing the copies by their frame \emph{mean} leaves the generated sample exactly unchanged, at the start or mid-generation, whereas copying inflates the variance of Gaussian static content by exactly $F$.
Both hold to float precision in a solvable model; with an exact nonlinear denoiser, copying returns the original clip in $\IdealCopySame\,\%$ of runs and averaging in $\IdealMeanSame$--$\IdealMeanSixSame\,\%$.
In a trained $1.3$M-parameter transformer, copying over-disperses backgrounds $\ACopyStd\times$ and lands $\ACopyOrig$\,dB from the original sample.
Averaging removes the over-dispersion and gains $13$\,dB, but a network that has never seen tied tokens over-produces texture, and on equal footing frame sharing then loses to simply taking fewer steps.
A short fine-tune with a regulariser that encodes the sufficiency of the frame mean closes the gap: with the base report's own 2-step draft map, mean-tied sharing reproduces the original sample at $\BMeanDraftOrig$\,dB with $\BDraftSpeedup\times$ fewer FLOPs and at $\BMeanAggrOrig$\,dB with $\BAggrSpeedup\times$ (oracle map: $\BMeanOrig$\,dB, $\BOracleSpeedup\times$), more than $15$\,dB above the fewer-steps baseline, while copying stays at $\BCopyOrig$\,dB.
The regulariser is not free: a no-regulariser control shows it, not extra training, causes the change, and it costs texture in the model's samples ($\VarFiveTex$ to $\VarBTex$ of the data's level, against $\VarATex$), a trade-off we chart but do not resolve.
All experiments are toy-scale and CPU-only.
